A weekly letter Weekly Recs October 18, 2026 · Autumn
"Things That Return Without Warning"

Autumn does this thing where it brings back feelings you forgot you had. Old anxieties, old certainties, the shape of someone's voice. This week is about recursion and recognition, how patterns come back changed but somehow the same.

Gerhard Richter
Abstraktes Bild (809-4)
Gerhard Richter · Abstraktes Bild (809-4)
Oil on canvas · Contemporary, 1994 · Art Institute of Chicago

Richter scrapes and layers paint until you can't tell what came first. Looks like sediment, like something geological happening in fast-forward. All these buried colours trying to surface through grey, which feels right for November starting.

Listen
Visitor New
Lullatone · 2014 · Start with "The Pajama Song"

Japanese-American duo making tiny electronic songs about everyday rituals. Sounds like CHAI's sweetness without the punk edge, music box melodies over glitchy beats. Perfect for grey mornings when you need something gentle but not sleepy.

Watch
After Life
Dir. Hirokazu Kore-eda · 1998

People at a way-station choose one memory to take into eternity. Shot documentary-style with non-actors recounting real memories. Fits that Japanese folk influence in your music taste, the way Ichiko Aoba songs feel like catching something dissolving.

Read
Die Ringe des Saturn
W. G. Sebald · 1995

A walking tour of Suffolk that spirals into meditations on silkworms, Thomas Browne, colonial violence, herring fishing. Sebald moves between topics the way memory actually works, one thing calling up another until you've travelled centuries without noticing.

Words
One Art
Elizabeth Bishop · 1976
The art of losing isn't hard to master; so many things seem filled with the intent to be lost that their loss is no disaster. Lose something every day. Accept the fluster of lost door keys, the hour badly spent. The art of losing isn't hard to master. Then practice losing farther, losing faster: places, and names, and where it was you meant to travel. None of these will bring disaster. I lost my mother's watch. And look! my last, or next-to-last, of three loved houses went. The art of losing isn't hard to master. I lost two cities, lovely ones. And, vaster, some realms I owned, two rivers, a continent. I miss them, but it wasn't a disaster. —Even losing you (the joking voice, a gesture I love) I shan't have lied. It's evident the art of losing's not too hard to master though it may look like (Write it!) like disaster.

Bishop lying to herself in real time, the way the villanelle form makes her circle back and admit more each time.

How it all connects

Bishop's villanelle keeps returning to the same line until it breaks. Richter scrapes back to earlier paint layers, making history visible. Kore-eda's film asks which moment you'd loop forever. Lullatone builds songs from repeated toy piano phrases. Sebald walks the same coastal path his narrator will die on. Everything here is about returning to something and finding it altered by the return itself.

Something to Read
The Rehearsal
Dan Chiasson · The New Yorker

On repetition, Elizabeth Bishop again, and what it means to practice something until it becomes true. Chiasson writes about how poets use repetition not for emphasis but for transformation, the way saying something again changes what it means.
